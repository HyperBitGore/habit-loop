Daily todo planning: a simple system that works

The classic todo list has a design flaw: it only grows. Everything you might ever do lives on one list, the list gets longer every week, and eventually opening it feels like being handed an invoice. The fix isn't a more elaborate system — it's giving every todo a date, and treating each day as its own short list.

Why dates beat one big list

A dated todo answers the only question that matters: "what am I doing today?" When today's list has five items instead of fifty, you can actually finish it — and finishing a list, even a small one, changes how the whole system feels. The master list of fifty items isn't gone; it's just distributed across days where each item has a fighting chance of getting done.

Plan tomorrow the night before

The highest-leverage five minutes in personal productivity is planning tomorrow before it starts. In the evening, while today is still fresh, pick the handful of things tomorrow actually needs. Morning planning sounds equivalent, but it isn't: by morning you're already in reactive mode, and the loudest task wins instead of the most important one. Deciding the night before means you start the day executing, not negotiating with yourself.

Three to five real todos

A day plan should contain roughly three to five meaningful items. More than that and you're writing fiction — the overflow won't get done, and routinely failing your own plan teaches you to ignore it. If something must happen tomorrow, it goes on the list; if it's merely nice, it goes on a later date. Recurring behaviors — exercise, reading, practice — shouldn't take up todo slots at all. They're habits: schedule them once and let the habit tracker handle the repetition.

Defer without guilt

Some todos won't get done. The worst response is letting them rot at the bottom of today's list as a standing reproach. The better move is an explicit defer: push the todo to tomorrow (or a specific later date) as a conscious decision. Deferring is honest re-planning — it keeps every day's list real. One warning sign to watch for: a todo you've deferred five times isn't a scheduling problem. Either it doesn't actually matter (delete it) or it's too big (cut it into a first step you'd genuinely do in one sitting).

Connect todos to goals

Daily lists keep you moving; goals keep you moving somewhere. A useful bridge is to write a goal as an ordered series of concrete steps, then schedule the current step as a dated todo. When the day's list includes "draft chapter outline" linked to the goal "finish the book," checking it off advances something larger than the day — and next steps never have to be rediscovered.

The whole system

  • Every evening, pick 3–5 todos for tomorrow.
  • Recurring behaviors are habits, not todos.
  • Didn't finish? Defer deliberately — never let items silently rot.
  • Deferred five times? Delete it or shrink it.
  • Link todos to goal steps so daily work compounds.
