How to start a new habit (and actually keep it)

Most new habits don't fail because of laziness. They fail because they were designed for the most motivated version of you — the one who decided, at 11 p.m. on a Sunday, to run five kilometers every morning. That person shows up for about four days. The habit has to survive the other days, when you're tired, busy, or simply not in the mood.

Start smaller than feels useful

The single most reliable trick in habit building is to shrink the habit until it's almost embarrassing. Not "read for an hour" but "read two pages." Not "go to the gym" but "put on gym shoes and walk out the door." A tiny habit feels pointless, which is exactly why it works: on a bad day, two pages is still doable, so the chain doesn't break. On a good day, nobody stops at two pages anyway.

You're not training the behavior at first — you're training the act of showing up. Volume can come later, once showing up is automatic.

Anchor it to something you already do

Habits need a trigger, and the most dependable triggers are routines you already have. "After I pour my morning coffee, I write one sentence in my journal." "After I brush my teeth, I do ten squats." This is often called habit stacking: the existing routine becomes the cue, so you never need to remember the new habit — your day reminds you.

Pick a realistic schedule, not a heroic one

Daily habits are easier to automate than "three times a week" habits, because there's never a decision about whether today counts. But not everything should be daily. Strength training needs rest days; a weekly review belongs on one specific weekday. The honest question is: what frequency would I keep during my worst normal week? Schedule that. You can always increase it once the habit is boringly stable.

Track it, honestly

Tracking does two things. It gives you a small, immediate reward — checking the box is genuinely satisfying — and it replaces the vague feeling of "I've been pretty good lately" with an actual record. The record matters most when it's unflattering: a calendar with gaps tells you precisely where your system breaks down. Maybe you never exercise on travel days, or every lapse starts on a weekend. That's design information, not a verdict on your character.

The one rule: never mark a habit done when it wasn't. A tracker you lie to is just a diary of wishes.

Expect the dip

Almost every new habit has a dip somewhere between day ten and day thirty, when novelty has worn off and automaticity hasn't arrived yet. This is normal, not a sign the habit is wrong for you. Plan for it: keep the habit tiny through the dip, and judge yourself on showing up, not on performance. Research on habit formation suggests automaticity takes anywhere from three weeks to several months depending on the behavior — so the only real deadline is "keep going."

Put it into practice

Pick one habit — just one — shrink it, anchor it to an existing routine, choose an honest schedule, and track it for a month.
